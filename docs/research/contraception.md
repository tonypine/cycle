# Contraception: how each method changes bleeding and estimates

She asked for Cycle to know whether she has an implant or takes the pill, because each one changes
how her cycle behaves. This note covers the methods Cycle should be able to record. For each one it
says whether she still ovulates, what her bleeding looks like in the first months and later,
whether a next-bleed estimate means anything, what happens when she stops, and which patterns are
worth mentioning to a doctor. It ends with what all this means for Cycle.

Cycle records the method she uses so its words and estimates fit her. It never acts as
contraception, never suggests choosing, starting, changing or stopping a method, never tells her
whether she is protected, and never calls a day safe ([`predictions.md`](predictions.md)). Decisions
about her method belong to her and her clinician.

## Three ideas that apply to every method

### A period, a withdrawal bleed, or bleeding

- **A period** is the bleed that follows ovulation, when progesterone from the ovary falls
  ([`cycle-physiology.md`](cycle-physiology.md)). Only cycles with ovulation have one.
- **A withdrawal bleed** is the bleed in the break of a combined pill, patch or ring, when the
  hormones she takes stop for a few days, and the bleed two to three weeks after a monthly combined
  injection, when its oestrogen falls. The FSRH says to make clear to users that "this bleed does
  not represent physiological menstruation" and has no health benefit
  ([FSRH CHC 2019, 6.1.1](https://www.cosrh.org/Common/Uploaded%20files/documents/fsrh-guideline-combined-hormonal-contraception-october-2023.pdf));
  ACOG calls the monthly withdrawal bleed a holdover from the pill's design that is not needed for
  health ([ACOG 2022](https://www.acog.org/clinical/clinical-guidance/clinical-consensus/articles/2022/09/general-approaches-to-medical-management-of-menstrual-suppression)).
  The NHS describes it as "bleeding like a period"
  ([NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/contraception/methods-of-contraception/combined-pill/how-to-take-it/)).
- **Bleeding** on a progestogen-only method (pill, implant, injection, hormonal IUD) is usually
  neither: the hormone thins the womb lining, and bleeding comes at times set by the method more
  than by a cycle
  ([WHO FP Handbook, implants](https://fphandbook.org/side-effects-health-benefits-and-health-risks-6),
  [FSRH bleeding 2015](https://www.cosrh.org/Common/Uploaded%20files/documents/ceuguidanceproblematicbleedinghormonalcontraception.pdf)).
  Even where she still ovulates (on a hormonal IUD or a traditional progestogen-only pill), the
  lining is changed, and a bleed cannot be told apart from bleeding the method causes
  ([FSRH IUC 2023](https://www.cosrh.org/Common/Uploaded%20files/documents/fsrh-clinical-guideline-intrauterine-contraception-mar-23-amended.pdf),
  [FSRH POP 2022](https://www.cosrh.org/Common/Uploaded%20files/documents/fsrh-ceu-clinical-guideline-progestogen-only-pills-aug22-amended-11july-2023-.pdf)).
  The WHO Handbook says "monthly bleeding" or "vaginal bleeding" for every method, never "period"
  ([WHO glossary](https://fphandbook.org/glossary)).

The copper IUD has no hormones: she keeps her own cycle, and her bleeds are periods.

### Describing bleeding that does not follow a cycle: 90-day reference periods

Cycle length means nothing when bleeding is not cyclic. Since 1986 the WHO has described bleeding
on contraception over 90-day **reference periods** instead
([Belsey 1986](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3539509/)), and the FSRH, the IUD and implant
guidelines and the Nexplanon label all use them. A **bleeding episode** is one or more days in a
row of bleeding or spotting, with bleed-free days on both sides
([FSRH bleeding 2015, Box 1](https://www.cosrh.org/Common/Uploaded%20files/documents/ceuguidanceproblematicbleedinghormonalcontraception.pdf)).

| Pattern in a 90-day reference period | Definition |
| -- | -- |
| Amenorrhoea (no bleeding) | No bleeding or spotting at all. |
| Infrequent bleeding | One or two bleeding or spotting episodes. |
| Normal frequency | Three to five episodes. |
| Frequent bleeding | More than five episodes. |
| Prolonged bleeding | An episode lasting more than 14 days (the FSRH's 2015 guidance and implant guideline say 14 days or more). |
| Irregular bleeding | Three to five episodes, with fewer than three bleed-free gaps of 14 days or more. |

Sources: [FSRH bleeding 2015](https://www.cosrh.org/Common/Uploaded%20files/documents/ceuguidanceproblematicbleedinghormonalcontraception.pdf),
[FSRH IUC 2023, Table 12](https://www.cosrh.org/Common/Uploaded%20files/documents/fsrh-clinical-guideline-intrauterine-contraception-mar-23-amended.pdf),
[FSRH implant 2021, Box 1](https://www.cosrh.org/Common/Uploaded%20files/documents/fsrh-guideline-progestogen-only-implants.pdf),
[Nexplanon label, Table 2](https://www.organon.com/product/usa/pi_circulars/n/nexplanon/nexplanon_pi.pdf).
Newer research guidance suggests counting per pack cycle for methods with a scheduled break, and
keeping 90-day periods for continuous ones
([Creinin 2022](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0010782422001524),
summarised in [Barretta 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12535263/)).

These are words for researchers and clinicians. In the app, the same facts read as plain counts
("bleeding or spotting on 12 days in the last 90, in 3 episodes"), not as labels.

### The first three months are different

Bleeding changes most in the first months on any method and often settles after. The FSRH uses
three months as the point after which persistent bleeding, or a change in bleeding, is worth an
examination, and says the cut-off is a guide only, because the implant and hormonal IUD often
cause bleeding after it ([FSRH bleeding 2015, 5.5.2](https://www.cosrh.org/Common/Uploaded%20files/documents/ceuguidanceproblematicbleedinghormonalcontraception.pdf)).
The NHS uses six months for the hormonal IUD
([NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/contraception/methods-of-contraception/ius-hormonal-coil/side-effects-and-risks/)).
So Cycle needs the date she started a method, not just its name.

## At a glance

| Method | Ovulation | What she logs | Next-bleed estimate | Fertile window, ovulation | Back to her own cycle after stopping |
| -- | -- | -- | -- | -- | -- |
| Combined pill with a break or dummy pills | Stopped | Withdrawal bleed in the break | Follows the pack | Hidden | Fast; first bleed after stopping is still a withdrawal bleed |
| Combined pill, extended or continuous | Stopped | Bleed in each break, if any; unscheduled bleeding | Only at a planned break; none if continuous | Hidden | Fast |
| Patch or ring | Stopped | Withdrawal bleed in week 4 | Follows the patch or ring weeks | Hidden | Fast (the NHS says 1 to 3 months) |
| Progestogen-only pill, traditional (levonorgestrel, norethisterone) | Not reliably stopped | Bleeding | None | Hidden | Fast |
| Progestogen-only pill, desogestrel or drospirenone | Stopped | Bleeding | None | Hidden | Fast |
| Implant | Stopped, nearly always | Bleeding | None | Hidden | Fast |
| Hormonal IUD | Usually continues | Bleeding | None by default | Hidden | Fast |
| Copper IUD | Continues | Periods | As without a method | As without a method | Immediate |
| Injection (DMPA, NET-EN) | Stopped | Bleeding | None | Hidden | Slow: months, up to a year |
| Monthly combined injection | Stopped | A withdrawal bleed after each injection; unscheduled bleeding | None by default | Hidden | Months: pregnancy about 5 months after the last injection on average |

Each row is detailed below, with sources.

## Combined pill

**Ovulation and what she logs.** Combined pills stop ovulation
([WHO FP Handbook](https://fphandbook.org/chapter-1-combined-oral-contraceptives)): the first seven
pills stop it and the rest keep it stopped. The bleed in the pill break or the placebo days is a
withdrawal bleed, not a period
([FSRH CHC 2019, 6.1.1](https://www.cosrh.org/Common/Uploaded%20files/documents/fsrh-guideline-combined-hormonal-contraception-october-2023.pdf)).

**Regimens.** A 21-day pill has 21 active pills and a 7-day break; an everyday (ED) pack fills the
break with 7, 4 or 2 dummy pills, and she bleeds on those instead
([NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/contraception/methods-of-contraception/combined-pill/how-to-take-it/)).
"Tailored" regimens shorten the break to 4 days, take three packs in a row
before a break (tricycling), run active pills until bleeding starts and then take a 4-day break
(flexible extended), or skip breaks altogether (continuous). The FSRH supports all of them and says
the traditional 21/7 bleed brings no health benefit
([FSRH CHC 2019, Table 1](https://www.cosrh.org/Common/Uploaded%20files/documents/fsrh-guideline-combined-hormonal-contraception-october-2023.pdf),
[NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/contraception/methods-of-contraception/combined-pill/how-to-take-it/),
[Devon Sexual Health, NHS](https://www.devonsexualhealth.nhs.uk/info-hub/taking-the-pill-a-different-approach-to-make-it-work-best-for-you/)).

**Bleeding.**

- First months: up to 20% have irregular bleeding in the first three months; it usually settles
  ([FSRH bleeding 2015, Table 1](https://www.cosrh.org/Common/Uploaded%20files/documents/ceuguidanceproblematicbleedinghormonalcontraception.pdf)).
  The WHO describes irregular bleeding for the first few months, then lighter and more regular
  bleeding ([WHO FP Handbook](https://fphandbook.org/chapter-1-combined-oral-contraceptives)).
- Later: a short, light withdrawal bleed in each break. Some users have no bleed in the break at
  all; the WHO says this is not harmful, and unlikely to mean pregnancy when every pill was taken
  ([WHO FP Handbook](https://fphandbook.org/managing-any-problems-4)). Pills with estradiol instead
  of ethinylestradiol bring shorter, lighter bleeds and more missing ones
  ([FSRH bleeding 2015](https://www.cosrh.org/Common/Uploaded%20files/documents/ceuguidanceproblematicbleedinghormonalcontraception.pdf)).
- Extended and continuous use: bleeding four times a year or not at all, but unscheduled bleeding
  is common and can last up to the first six months
  ([WHO FP Handbook](https://fphandbook.org/extended-and-continuous-use-combined-oral-contraceptives),
  [FSRH CHC 2019](https://www.cosrh.org/Common/Uploaded%20files/documents/fsrh-guideline-combined-hormonal-contraception-october-2023.pdf)).
  Unscheduled bleeding on a combined method is called breakthrough bleeding.

**Predictions.** The bleed follows the pack, not the body: on a 21-day or ED pill it comes in the
break or on the dummy pills, and the break is set by when she started the pack. So
a next-bleed estimate means something, in a different form: "bleed expected in your pill break",
not "period expected around". On tricycling it comes every nine weeks; on flexible extended use it
comes when she decides to take a break; on continuous use there is nothing to predict. Fertile
window and ovulation estimates mean nothing on any combined method.

**Stopping.** Fertility returns with no significant delay
([FSRH CHC 2019, 15.3.1](https://www.cosrh.org/Common/Uploaded%20files/documents/fsrh-guideline-combined-hormonal-contraception-october-2023.pdf),
[WHO FP Handbook](https://fphandbook.org/chapter-1-combined-oral-contraceptives)); the NHS says
about a month ([NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/contraception/methods-of-contraception/combined-pill/what-is-it/)).
After continuous use, 98.9% of 187 users had a natural period or a pregnancy within 90 days, and in
another study all but one of 47 ovulated within 37 days
([FSRH CHC 2019, 6.2.6](https://www.cosrh.org/Common/Uploaded%20files/documents/fsrh-guideline-combined-hormonal-contraception-october-2023.pdf)).
The bleed in the days after the last active pill is still a withdrawal bleed; her first natural
cycle starts with the period after it. After a hormonal method, the WHO delays its calendar-based
Standard Days Method "until the start of her next monthly bleeding"
([WHO FP Handbook, ch. 18](https://fphandbook.org/sites/default/files/Chapter_18_Eng.pdf)).

**For Cycle.** Estimate the next bleed from the pack (regimen and pack start date) if she records
them, or from her logged bleeds, which follow the pack; call it a bleed; hide the fertile window and
ovulation; on continuous use, show no next bleed.

## Patch and ring

**Ovulation and what she logs.** Both release the same two hormones as the combined pill and stop
ovulation. A patch is changed weekly for three weeks, a ring stays in for three weeks, and week four
is free, "to allow a withdrawal bleed"
([FSRH CHC 2019, 6.1.2–6.1.3](https://www.cosrh.org/Common/Uploaded%20files/documents/fsrh-guideline-combined-hormonal-contraception-october-2023.pdf),
[WHO FP Handbook, patch](https://fphandbook.org/chapter-6-combined-patch-only-essentials),
[ring](https://fphandbook.org/chapter-7-combined-vaginal-ring-only-essentials)). The tailored
regimens above apply to them too.

**Bleeding.** Irregular for the first few months, then lighter and more regular
([WHO FP Handbook, patch](https://fphandbook.org/chapter-6-combined-patch-only-essentials),
[ring](https://fphandbook.org/chapter-7-combined-vaginal-ring-only-essentials)). Bleeding on the
patch is like the pill's; the ring may bring less unscheduled bleeding
([FSRH bleeding 2015](https://www.cosrh.org/Common/Uploaded%20files/documents/ceuguidanceproblematicbleedinghormonalcontraception.pdf)).

**Predictions.** As for the combined pill: the bleed follows the patch-free or ring-free week.

**Stopping.** As after the pill, fertility returns with no significant delay after any combined
hormonal method
([FSRH CHC 2019, 15.3.1](https://www.cosrh.org/Common/Uploaded%20files/documents/fsrh-guideline-combined-hormonal-contraception-october-2023.pdf)).
The NHS puts the upper end in plain words: fertility usually returns within one to three months
([NHS patch](https://www.nhs.uk/contraception/methods-of-contraception/contraceptive-patch/what-is-it/),
[NHS ring](https://www.nhs.uk/contraception/methods-of-contraception/vaginal-ring/what-is-it/)).
The bleed after the last patch or ring is still a withdrawal bleed, as after the last active pill.

**For Cycle.** The same as the combined pill, with "patch-free week" or "ring-free week" in place
of "pill break".

## Progestogen-only pill

There are three kinds, and they differ in what matters here.

**Ovulation and what she logs.**

- **Traditional pills** (levonorgestrel 30 µg, norethisterone 350 µg) do not reliably stop
  ovulation: in one trial, 28% of treatment cycles on levonorgestrel had one. They work mostly
  through cervical mucus
  ([FSRH POP 2022](https://www.cosrh.org/Common/Uploaded%20files/documents/fsrh-ceu-clinical-guideline-progestogen-only-pills-aug22-amended-11july-2023-.pdf)).
- **Desogestrel 75 µg** stops ovulation (1.7% of treatment cycles in the same
  trial), and so does **drospirenone 4 mg**, taken as 24 active and 4 placebo pills
  ([FSRH POP 2022](https://www.cosrh.org/Common/Uploaded%20files/documents/fsrh-ceu-clinical-guideline-progestogen-only-pills-aug22-amended-11july-2023-.pdf)).

None has a break meant to bring a bleed, except drospirenone's four placebo days, and even then a
bleed may or may not come. What she logs on any of them is bleeding, not a period.

**Bleeding.** Unpredictable, and it may not settle with time
([FSRH bleeding 2015](https://www.cosrh.org/Common/Uploaded%20files/documents/ceuguidanceproblematicbleedinghormonalcontraception.pdf)).
Over a 90-day period at around a year of use
([FSRH POP 2022, 12.1](https://www.cosrh.org/Common/Uploaded%20files/documents/fsrh-ceu-clinical-guideline-progestogen-only-pills-aug22-amended-11july-2023-.pdf)):

| Pattern | Levonorgestrel | Desogestrel | Drospirenone (at 6 to 9 months) |
| -- | -- | -- | -- |
| No bleeding | About 2% | 2 to 3 in 10 | 2 to 3 in 10 |
| Infrequent | About 1 in 10 | About 3 in 10 | Not given |
| Normal frequency | About 8 in 10 | About 4 in 10 | Not given |
| Frequent | About 1 in 10 | Fewer than 1 in 10 | Fewer than 1 in 10 |
| Prolonged | Fewer than 1 in 10 | About 1 in 10 | Fewer than 1 in 10 |

The WHO and NHS list lighter, more frequent, irregular or absent bleeding and spotting between
bleeds ([WHO FP Handbook](https://fphandbook.org/side-effects-health-benefits-and-health-risks-12),
[NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/contraception/methods-of-contraception/progestogen-only-pill/side-effects-and-risks/)).
In breastfeeding users, the pill lengthens the time without bleeding after birth
([WHO FP Handbook](https://fphandbook.org/chapter-2-progestin-only-pills)).

**Predictions.** No next-bleed date: the bleeding does not follow the pill pack, and on desogestrel
or drospirenone it does not follow a cycle either. On a traditional pill her bleeds may stay
cycle-like (about 8 in 10 have three to five episodes in 90 days), but the FSRH still calls the
pattern unpredictable.

**Stopping.** No delay in the return of fertility
([FSRH POP 2022](https://www.cosrh.org/Common/Uploaded%20files/documents/fsrh-ceu-clinical-guideline-progestogen-only-pills-aug22-amended-11july-2023-.pdf),
[WHO FP Handbook](https://fphandbook.org/chapter-2-progestin-only-pills)); the NHS says about a
month ([NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/contraception/methods-of-contraception/progestogen-only-pill/what-is-it/)).
After desogestrel, ovulation came back 7 to 30 days after the last pill, 17 days on average
([FSRH POP 2022](https://www.cosrh.org/Common/Uploaded%20files/documents/fsrh-ceu-clinical-guideline-progestogen-only-pills-aug22-amended-11july-2023-.pdf)).

**For Cycle.** No next-bleed estimate; call it bleeding; hide the fertile window and ovulation; show
what she logged as a 90-day summary.

## Implant

Nexplanon (also called Implanon NXT; it replaced Implanon) is one rod in the upper arm, releasing
etonogestrel, labelled for three years. Jadelle and Levoplant are two-rod
levonorgestrel implants used elsewhere
([WHO FP Handbook](https://fphandbook.org/chapter-9-implants)).

**Ovulation and what she logs.** Etonogestrel reaches levels that stop ovulation within a day of
insertion. During use, ovulation is infrequent, though the ovaries are not fully quiet: follicles
still grow and oestrogen does not fall below early-cycle levels
([FSRH implant 2021](https://www.cosrh.org/Common/Uploaded%20files/documents/fsrh-guideline-progestogen-only-implants.pdf)).
What she logs is bleeding, not a period.

**Bleeding.** The widest mix of any method, and "bleeding pattern may change at any time"
([FSRH implant 2021](https://www.cosrh.org/Common/Uploaded%20files/documents/fsrh-guideline-progestogen-only-implants.pdf)).

- First months to a year: lighter, prolonged, irregular, infrequent or no bleeding; implant users
  are more likely to have infrequent, prolonged or no bleeding than irregular bleeding
  ([WHO FP Handbook](https://fphandbook.org/side-effects-health-benefits-and-health-risks-6)).
  A third of users have prolonged bleeding in the first few months
  ([FSRH implant 2021](https://www.cosrh.org/Common/Uploaded%20files/documents/fsrh-guideline-progestogen-only-implants.pdf)).
- Later, in 90-day periods from 3 to 24 months
  ([Nexplanon label, 5.3](https://www.organon.com/product/usa/pi_circulars/n/nexplanon/nexplanon_pi.pdf),
  [FSRH implant 2021](https://www.cosrh.org/Common/Uploaded%20files/documents/fsrh-guideline-progestogen-only-implants.pdf)):

  | Pattern in a 90-day period | Share of 90-day periods |
  | -- | -- |
  | Infrequent (fewer than 3 episodes) | 33.6% |
  | No bleeding | 22.2% |
  | Prolonged (an episode over 14 days) | 17.7% |
  | Frequent (more than 5 episodes) | 6.7% |

  On average, 17.7 days of bleeding or spotting every 90 days, in about 2.4 episodes. In 75% of
  90-day periods the number of bleeding days is no more than in a natural cycle, but the days come
  at unpredictable times ([FSRH bleeding 2015](https://www.cosrh.org/Common/Uploaded%20files/documents/ceuguidanceproblematicbleedinghormonalcontraception.pdf)).
  No bleeding peaks at about 25% of users at 6 to 9 months and falls to 12% by three years
  ([FSRH implant 2021](https://www.cosrh.org/Common/Uploaded%20files/documents/fsrh-guideline-progestogen-only-implants.pdf)).
- Bleeding changes were the commonest reason to have the implant taken out in trials (11.1%),
  irregular bleeding above all ([Nexplanon label](https://www.organon.com/product/usa/pi_circulars/n/nexplanon/nexplanon_pi.pdf)).
- The NHS's plain-language version: bleeding may be lighter, less regular with spotting, heavier or
  more painful, longer, or stop; or unpredictable, "a heavy period followed by a very light period,
  or missing a period"
  ([NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/contraception/methods-of-contraception/contraceptive-implant/side-effects-and-risks/)).

**Predictions.** No dates. Implant bleeding does not follow a cycle, so there is no cycle length to
average and no next bleed to expect. What can be said is about the pattern: the first three months
are "broadly predictive" of later bleeding for many users
([Nexplanon label](https://www.organon.com/product/usa/pi_circulars/n/nexplanon/nexplanon_pi.pdf)).
Users with a favourable pattern (no more than five episodes in 90 days, none of 14 days or more)
have about an 80% chance of keeping it in the next 90 days, and those with an unfavourable one about
a 50% chance that it improves over time
([FSRH implant 2021, 11.1.3](https://www.cosrh.org/Common/Uploaded%20files/documents/fsrh-guideline-progestogen-only-implants.pdf)).
That is counselling for clinicians, not a forecast to show; Cycle can show her own last 90 days and
leave the rest out.

**Stopping.** Fast. Etonogestrel is undetectable within a week of removal, almost everyone ovulates
within six weeks, and pregnancies have started within 7 to 14 days
([FSRH implant 2021](https://www.cosrh.org/Common/Uploaded%20files/documents/fsrh-guideline-progestogen-only-implants.pdf),
[Nexplanon label, 5.14](https://www.organon.com/product/usa/pi_circulars/n/nexplanon/nexplanon_pi.pdf)).
The bleeding pattern she had before the implant generally returns after removal
([WHO FP Handbook](https://fphandbook.org/questions-and-answers-about-implants)). The first bleed
after removal starts her first natural cycle.

**For Cycle.** No next-bleed estimate and no cycle-day counter; call it bleeding; hide the fertile
window and ovulation; show a 90-day summary of what she logged; when the implant comes out, count
natural cycles again from the first period after.

## Hormonal IUD

A T-shaped device in the womb releasing levonorgestrel: 52 mg (Mirena, Levosert, Liletta), 19.5 mg
(Kyleena) or 13.5 mg (Jaydess)
([WHO FP Handbook](https://fphandbook.org/chapter-11-levonorgestrel-intrauterine-device),
[FSRH IUC 2023](https://www.cosrh.org/Common/Uploaded%20files/documents/fsrh-clinical-guideline-intrauterine-contraception-mar-23-amended.pdf)).

**Ovulation and what she logs.** It works mainly on the womb lining and cervical mucus. Most users,
more than 75%, keep ovulating, and more so on the smaller doses: in the first year, 77% on 52 mg,
89% on 19.5 mg and 97% on 13.5 mg
([FSRH IUC 2023](https://www.cosrh.org/Common/Uploaded%20files/documents/fsrh-clinical-guideline-intrauterine-contraception-mar-23-amended.pdf)).
So she may still have an ovulatory cycle underneath, while the bleeding itself is changed by the
device in the womb. What she logs is bleeding.

**Bleeding.**

- First months: frequent, prolonged or irregular bleeding and spotting are common for three to six
  months. In the first 90 days on 52 mg, 51% had prolonged and 26% frequent bleeding
  ([FSRH IUC 2023, Table 13](https://www.cosrh.org/Common/Uploaded%20files/documents/fsrh-clinical-guideline-intrauterine-contraception-mar-23-amended.pdf),
  [NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/contraception/methods-of-contraception/ius-hormonal-coil/side-effects-and-risks/)).
- Later: bleeding falls steadily, from about 36 days of bleeding or spotting in the first 90 days to
  12 by the end of the first year on 52 mg. By the last 90 days of the first year, 20% have no
  bleeding and 31% infrequent bleeding on 52 mg; 12% and 26% on 19.5 mg; 6 to 9% and 19 to 20% on
  13.5 mg. By the end of licensed use, 34 to 39% on 52 mg have no bleeding
  ([FSRH IUC 2023, Tables 13–14](https://www.cosrh.org/Common/Uploaded%20files/documents/fsrh-clinical-guideline-intrauterine-contraception-mar-23-amended.pdf)).
  Blood loss falls by about 90% over a year on 52 mg, which is why it also treats heavy periods
  ([FSRH bleeding 2015](https://www.cosrh.org/Common/Uploaded%20files/documents/ceuguidanceproblematicbleedinghormonalcontraception.pdf)).
- A replacement device brings a small, short rise in bleeding in its first 90 days
  ([FSRH IUC 2023](https://www.cosrh.org/Common/Uploaded%20files/documents/fsrh-clinical-guideline-intrauterine-contraception-mar-23-amended.pdf)).

**Predictions.** None by default. In the first six months there is no pattern to predict. Later,
some users bleed lightly at regular intervals because they still ovulate, and a next-bleed estimate
from her own logged bleeds could then make sense, labelled like any estimate; whether to offer it is
a design question for [MOT-50](https://linear.app/tonypine/issue/MOT-50). Fertility estimates stay
hidden: ovulation may happen, but estimating it on a contraceptive would invite the "safe day"
reading Cycle must never give.

**Stopping.** Fertility returns straight away
([NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/contraception/methods-of-contraception/ius-hormonal-coil/what-is-it/)),
and most users were ovulating all along, so periods usually come back with her next cycle.

**For Cycle.** No next-bleed estimate by default; call it bleeding; hide the fertile window and
ovulation; show a 90-day summary; expect a lot of bleeding in the first months and say so calmly.

## Copper IUD

**Ovulation and what she logs.** No hormones: it works by a chemical change that damages sperm and
egg ([WHO FP Handbook](https://fphandbook.org/chapter-10-copper-bearing-intrauterine-device)). She
ovulates as before, and her bleeds are periods.

**Bleeding.** Periods are often longer, heavier and more painful, especially in the first three to
six months ([WHO FP Handbook](https://fphandbook.org/side-effects-health-benefits-health-risks-and-complications)).
The heavier bleeding often eases with time; spotting between periods is less likely to
([FSRH IUC 2023, 9.1.1](https://www.cosrh.org/Common/Uploaded%20files/documents/fsrh-clinical-guideline-intrauterine-contraception-mar-23-amended.pdf),
[NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/contraception/methods-of-contraception/iud-coil/side-effects/)).

**Predictions.** The same as without a method: her cycle length is her own. Expected period length
should come from periods logged since insertion, because they may be longer than before. Whether the
fertile window and ovulation appear follows her answer to the first open question in
[`product-implications.md`](product-implications.md), as without a method, and never with any
suggestion about protection.

**Stopping.** Fertility returns straight away
([NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/contraception/methods-of-contraception/iud-coil/what-is-it/),
[WHO FP Handbook](https://fphandbook.org/questions-and-answers-about-intrauterine-device)).

**For Cycle.** Estimate as without a method; call them periods; base the expected period length on
periods since insertion; keep these cycles in her history and predictions; flag long periods at more
than 8 days, from three months after insertion
([Worth mentioning to a doctor, on a method](#worth-mentioning-to-a-doctor-on-a-method)).

## Injection

The common injection is DMPA (medroxyprogesterone acetate): Depo-Provera into the muscle, or Sayana
Press under the skin, every 13 weeks. NET-EN (Noristerat) is given every 8 weeks
([WHO FP Handbook](https://fphandbook.org/chapter-4-progestin-only-injectables)). Both are
progestogen only. The monthly combined injection, which adds an oestrogen, behaves differently and
has [its own section](#monthly-combined-injection).

**Ovulation and what she logs.** Injections stop ovulation
([WHO FP Handbook](https://fphandbook.org/chapter-4-progestin-only-injectables)). What she logs is
bleeding.

**Bleeding.**

- First three months: irregular and prolonged bleeding are common; about 1 in 10 has none
  ([WHO FP Handbook](https://fphandbook.org/side-effects-health-benefits-and-health-risks-10),
  [FSRH bleeding 2015](https://www.cosrh.org/Common/Uploaded%20files/documents/ceuguidanceproblematicbleedinghormonalcontraception.pdf)).
- Later: less bleeding the longer she uses it. In a trial, no bleeding in a 90-day period rose from
  10% at three months to 47% at a year on the 150 mg dose
  ([FSRH injectable 2014](https://www.cosrh.org/Common/Uploaded%20files/documents/progestogen-only-injectable-december-2014-amended-11july2023.pdf));
  around half or more have none at 12 months
  ([FSRH bleeding 2015](https://www.cosrh.org/Common/Uploaded%20files/documents/ceuguidanceproblematicbleedinghormonalcontraception.pdf),
  [NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/contraception/methods-of-contraception/contraceptive-injection/side-effects-and-risks/)).
  NET-EN affects bleeding less than DMPA
  ([WHO FP Handbook](https://fphandbook.org/side-effects-health-benefits-and-health-risks-10)).

**Predictions.** No next-bleed date. The useful date on an injection is the next injection, which
she knows from her clinic; a reminder for it is something she could ask for, like a pill reminder.

**Stopping.** Slow, and the one method where a long gap is expected. After a single injection,
ovulation returned at a median of 183 days (into the muscle) or 212 days (under the skin)
([FSRH injectable 2014, 11.1](https://www.cosrh.org/Common/Uploaded%20files/documents/progestogen-only-injectable-december-2014-amended-11july2023.pdf)).
Users of DMPA conceive on average about 10 months after their last injection, four months later
than after other methods, and the length of use makes no difference
([WHO FP Handbook](https://fphandbook.org/questions-and-answers-about-progestin-only-injectables)).
Fertility can take up to a year to return
([FSRH injectable 2014](https://www.cosrh.org/Common/Uploaded%20files/documents/progestogen-only-injectable-december-2014-amended-11july2023.pdf),
[NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/contraception/methods-of-contraception/contraceptive-injection/what-is-it/)).
Her earlier bleeding pattern generally returns several months after the last injection, and she
may ovulate before her first bleed
([WHO FP Handbook](https://fphandbook.org/questions-and-answers-about-progestin-only-injectables)).
For its Standard Days Method, the WHO waits until the next injection would have been due and then
for the next bleed ([WHO FP Handbook, ch. 18](https://fphandbook.org/sites/default/files/Chapter_18_Eng.pdf)).

**For Cycle.** No next-bleed estimate; call it bleeding; hide the fertile window and ovulation;
after the last injection, do not treat months without bleeding as a missed period, and count natural
cycles from the first period after the injection would have run out.

## Monthly combined injection

A progestogen and an oestrogen, injected into the muscle every 4 weeks, up to 7 days early or late.
There are two: medroxyprogesterone acetate 25 mg with estradiol cypionate 5 mg (Cyclofem,
Ciclofemina, Cyclo-Provera, Lunelle, Novafem and other names) and norethisterone enanthate 50 mg
with estradiol valerate 5 mg (Mesigyna, Norigynon). The WHO says what it knows may also apply to
older formulations, about which less is known
([WHO FP Handbook, ch. 5](https://fphandbook.org/sites/default/files/JHU%20HBk22%20-%20Chapter%205.pdf)):
dihydroxyprogesterone (algestone) acetophenide with estradiol enanthate (Perlutan, Topasel,
Uno-Ciclo and other names), sold in many Latin American countries and Spain, and Chinese Injectable
No. 1 ([d'Arcangues and Snow, GFMER](https://www.gfmer.ch/Endo/Lectures_11/Arcangues.htm)).
Mesigyna has the same progestogen as Noristerat, at a lower dose and with an oestrogen; the method
list should not let one pass for the other.

**Where she might meet it.** Not in the UK, where the NHS lists only progestogen-only injections
([NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/contraception/methods-of-contraception/contraceptive-injection/what-is-it/)),
nor in the US, where Lunelle has not been sold since 2003
([Kaunitz, GLOWM 2008](https://www.glowm.com/section-view/heading/Injectable%20Contraception/item/392)).
Injectables are little known in Europe; Cyclofem was registered in 18 countries and Mesigyna in 35
([d'Arcangues and Snow, GFMER](https://www.gfmer.ch/Endo/Lectures_11/Arcangues.htm)). Whether
Cycle's method list needs it depends on where she lives and gets her contraception.

**Ovulation and what she logs.** It stops ovulation, and works much like the combined pill
([WHO FP Handbook, ch. 5](https://fphandbook.org/sites/default/files/JHU%20HBk22%20-%20Chapter%205.pdf)).
The oestrogen level falls about two weeks after each injection, and a withdrawal bleed follows
([d'Arcangues and Snow, GFMER](https://www.gfmer.ch/Endo/Lectures_11/Arcangues.htm)); the WHO and
the Lunelle label both call it a withdrawal bleed
([WHO FP Handbook, ch. 5, Q6](https://fphandbook.org/sites/default/files/JHU%20HBk22%20-%20Chapter%205.pdf),
[Lunelle label](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/label/2000/20874lbl.pdf)). So what
she logs is a bleed after each injection, set by the injection rather than by a cycle, and
sometimes bleeding at other times.

**Bleeding.** More regular than on the progestogen-only injection, because of the oestrogen and the
lower progestogen dose
([WHO FP Handbook, ch. 5, Q1](https://fphandbook.org/sites/default/files/JHU%20HBk22%20-%20Chapter%205.pdf),
[Gallo 2008, Cochrane](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18843662/)).

- When the bleed comes: 14 to 20 days after the first injection in a trial of over 5,000 users in
  China ([Sang 1995](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7621686/)); 20 to 25 days after (median 21 to
  22) for about half of users in the US Lunelle trial
  ([Lunelle label](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/label/2000/20874lbl.pdf)).
- First months: more bleeding days and episodes, and more variation, in the first 90 days than
  later ([Sang 1995](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7621686/)). In the first injection interval the
  bleed lasted more than 7 days for 42% of Lunelle users
  ([Lunelle label](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/label/2000/20874lbl.pdf)). The WHO
  lists lighter and fewer days of bleeding, irregular, infrequent or prolonged bleeding, or none,
  and says the changes usually lessen or stop within the first few months
  ([WHO FP Handbook, ch. 5](https://fphandbook.org/sites/default/files/JHU%20HBk22%20-%20Chapter%205.pdf)).
- Later: in any one injection interval, about 75% of users had a single bleed and nothing else, 15%
  no bleed, and 10% bleeding or spotting at other times. At a year, 29% still had a bleed of more
  than 7 days. Irregular bleeding stayed at about 30% through the first year, and only 4.1% had no
  bleeding at all ([Lunelle label](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/label/2000/20874lbl.pdf)).
  About two-thirds settle into one bleed a month and a third have irregular or prolonged bleeding
  ([d'Arcangues and Snow, GFMER](https://www.gfmer.ch/Endo/Lectures_11/Arcangues.htm)). The
  norethisterone version brings more regular and fewer prolonged bleeds than the
  medroxyprogesterone one ([Gallo 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18843662/),
  [Sang 1995](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7621686/)).

**Predictions.** The bleed follows the injection, as the combined pill's follows the pack, so
there is something to estimate, but less precisely: the sources put it anywhere from 14 to 25 days
after the injection, and a third of users bleed irregularly. The injection itself must not follow
the bleeding: the WHO and the label say to give it every 4 weeks whatever the bleeding does
([WHO FP Handbook, ch. 5, Q5](https://fphandbook.org/sites/default/files/JHU%20HBk22%20-%20Chapter%205.pdf),
[Lunelle label](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/label/2000/20874lbl.pdf)). So, as
for the hormonal IUD, no next bleed by default; whether to offer one, counted from her injection
date once her own logged bleeds follow her injections, is a design question for
[MOT-50](https://linear.app/tonypine/issue/MOT-50). The useful date is the next injection, which
she knows from her clinic. Fertile window and ovulation estimates mean nothing.

**Stopping.** Slower than the pill, much faster than DMPA. Users become pregnant on average about
5 months after the last injection, about one month later than after most other methods, and the
WHO says not being pregnant even 12 months after stopping is no cause for worry.
Her earlier bleeding pattern generally returns a few months after the last injection, and she may
ovulate before her first bleed
([WHO FP Handbook, ch. 5, Q10–11](https://fphandbook.org/sites/default/files/JHU%20HBk22%20-%20Chapter%205.pdf)).
In small studies, 52% ovulated in the first month after three months of Lunelle and 71% by the
second, 60% by the third month after two years of use, and in another study 11 of 14 women
ovulated 63 to 112 days after their third and last injection
([Lunelle label](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/label/2000/20874lbl.pdf)). Of 70
women who stopped Cyclofem to conceive, more than half were pregnant at 6 months and 82.9% at 12
([d'Arcangues and Snow, GFMER](https://www.gfmer.ch/Endo/Lectures_11/Arcangues.htm)). The bleed two
to three weeks after the last injection is still a withdrawal bleed.

**For Cycle.** No next-bleed estimate by default (MOT-50 decides whether to estimate one from the
injection date); call the bleed after each injection a bleed, and anything else bleeding; hide the
fertile window, ovulation and cycle day; show what she logged and a 90-day summary; count natural
cycles from the first period after the next injection would have been due.

## Stopping a method, in numbers

How quickly people who stopped a method to conceive became pregnant within a year, pooled from 22
studies: 87% after the pill, 85% after an IUD, 78% after an injection and 75% after an implant (83%
without one outlying study), 83% overall
([Girum 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6055351/)). The authors found no significant
difference between hormonal methods and IUDs at a year. The difference is in how soon: weeks for
most methods, months for the injections (about 10 months on average after DMPA, about 5 after the
monthly combined injection).

## Worth mentioning to a doctor, on a method

The same house rules as [`health-signals.md`](health-signals.md): describe what she logged, then
suggest that it is worth mentioning to a doctor or nurse; never name a condition as a cause; once,
calmly, dismissible. Clinicians assessing bleeding on contraception check for causes unrelated to
the method, including a pregnancy test
([FSRH bleeding 2015](https://www.cosrh.org/Common/Uploaded%20files/documents/ceuguidanceproblematicbleedinghormonalcontraception.pdf));
that is their job, so Cycle does not prompt pregnancy tests (see the copy rules in
[`product-implications.md`](product-implications.md)).

On a hormonal method, the natural-cycle signals about cycle length, regularity and a long time
without a period do not apply: those patterns are what the method does. These apply instead.

With a copper IUD the natural-cycle signals in [`health-signals.md`](health-signals.md) apply from
the day it goes in, except the long-period one: it uses the WHO's 8 days instead of 7, and waits
until three months after insertion, because longer periods are expected in the first months
([WHO FP Handbook, copper IUD](https://fphandbook.org/managing-any-problems)). So a 7- or 8-day
period on a copper IUD raises no card, and neither does a longer one in the first three months. The
signs of heavy bleeding (soaking through in an hour or two, large clots) apply at any time.

## What this means for Cycle

- **Record the method, never advise on it.** A contraception setting holds her method and the dates
  she started and stopped it, as date ranges like the other life stages
  ([`tracking-data.md`](tracking-data.md)). For combined methods, optionally the regimen (21 days
  and a break, an everyday pack with dummy pills, a shortened break, tricycling, flexible,
  continuous) and the date her current pack, patch cycle or ring started, or of her last monthly
  injection. No method recommendations, no comparisons, no "should I stop?" content, no missed-pill
  or protection advice, no safe days.
- **The words follow the method.** "Period" without a method or with a copper IUD; "bleed" for the
  scheduled bleed on a combined pill, patch or ring; "bleeding" on progestogen-only methods and for
  unscheduled bleeding on combined ones; on the monthly combined injection, "bleed" for the bleed
  after each injection and "bleeding" for the rest. The log itself does not change: bleeding days,
  flow and spotting are stored the same way, and only the labels and the estimates built on them
  differ.
- **What to estimate, hide and say, per method:**

  | Method | Estimate | Hide | Say |
  | -- | -- | -- | -- |
  | Combined pill with a break or dummy pills, patch, ring | Next bleed, in the break | Fertile window, ovulation, phases | "Bleed expected in your pill break, from 12 to 15 May" |
  | Combined, tricycling or flexible | Next bleed only at a planned break | Fertile window, ovulation, phases | "Next planned break: 3 to 6 June" |
  | Combined, continuous | Nothing | Next bleed, fertile window, ovulation, phases | What she logged |
  | Progestogen-only pill | Nothing | Next bleed, cycle day, fertile window, ovulation, phases | A 90-day summary |
  | Implant | Nothing | Next bleed, cycle day, fertile window, ovulation, phases | A 90-day summary |
  | Hormonal IUD | Nothing by default; maybe the next bleed once her own bleeds are regular (a design question for MOT-50) | Next bleed (by default), cycle day, fertile window, ovulation, phases | A 90-day summary |
  | Copper IUD | As without a method | As without a method | Periods, as without a method |
  | Injection (DMPA, NET-EN) | Nothing (a next-injection reminder only if she asks) | Next bleed, cycle day, fertile window, ovulation, phases | A 90-day summary |
  | Monthly combined injection | Nothing by default; maybe the next bleed from her injection date once her own bleeds follow it (a design question for MOT-50); a next-injection reminder only if she asks | Next bleed (by default), cycle day, fertile window, ovulation, phases | What she logged and a 90-day summary |

- **A 90-day summary instead of cycle lengths** on progestogen-only methods and the monthly
  combined injection: days of bleeding or spotting and the number of episodes in the last 90 days,
  and the longest episode, in plain words. It follows the WHO's reference periods, so it is also
  what a clinician will ask about.
- **Her natural-cycle numbers stay clean.** Cycles on a hormonal method are left out of her median
  cycle and period length and her prediction ranges, as [`predictions.md`](predictions.md) already
  says; the days stay in her log and history. Copper IUD cycles count.
- **Starting a hormonal method** ends the natural-cycle predictions from the start date; cycle-day
  counts, "your period could start any day now", "Missed a period?" and late-period messages
  assume a natural cycle and do not apply.
- **Stopping a method** ends that mode on the stop date. On a combined method, the bleed just after
  stopping is still a withdrawal bleed. Natural cycles count from the first period after it (after
  either injection, from the first period after the next injection would have been due). Until she
  has logged a complete cycle, predictions use her usual lengths with the wide little-data range,
  and say why: there are no natural cycles logged since she stopped.
- **The first months on a method are noisy.** Signals about bleeding on a method wait for three
  months after the start date (six for the hormonal IUD), except bleeding after sex and the signs of
  heavy bleeding in [`health-signals.md`](health-signals.md) (soaking through in an hour or two,
  large clots), which do not wait. On a copper IUD only the long-period signal waits, and it uses 8
  days instead of 7; the other natural-cycle signals apply from the start.
- **Health signals change with the method**, as listed above. The thresholds and the method they
  apply to belong in one place in code with their sources, like the others.
- **Urgent symptoms are reference text, not alerts.** The method screen of the combined pill, patch,
  ring and both IUDs ends with "When to get help", the same for every user of the method and never
  set off by her log ([`0007`](../decisions/0007-urgent-symptoms-on-a-method.md)).
- **Synthetic data only.** Every example here uses made-up dates and numbers, like the rest of the
  repo.
